Wildfire susceptibility maps are a common input to prevention planning, and most of them are trained
and evaluated in a single region. Agencies without a long local fire record, for example after a first
large fire, may have to use a map developed elsewhere. We tested whether such maps transfer between
regions, using five large Mediterranean fire seasons of 2021 and 2022 in Manavgat and Muğla (Türkiye),
Bejís (Spain), North Evia (Greece) and Montiferru (Italy).

The manuscript reports three results on how susceptibility models are evaluated and used. First, the
area on which a model is scored changes its apparent skill, and the same models lost 0.160 ROC-AUC
when scored on the burn scar and a 2 km collar instead of region-wide. Second, pre-fire satellite land
surface temperature and dryness predictors improved susceptibility within every region (+0.045 to
+0.148 ROC-AUC at 5 km blocking), but the gain carried to other regions was close to zero (+0.007
[−0.020, +0.038]), and none of twenty similarity measures told in advance which transfers would work.
Third, hotter surfaces burned less in four of five regions, which is consistent with land surface
temperature describing the land surface more than fuel dryness. All intervals are spatial-block
bootstrap intervals. Each region contributes one fire season, which the manuscript states as a
limitation. For hazard practice, a susceptibility model trained elsewhere should be tested against
burned areas in the target region before it guides prevention planning, and every reported score
should name the area it refers to.

*Natural Hazards* has published machine-learning wildfire susceptibility maps, for example Thies
(2025, https://doi.org/10.1007/s11069-025-07292-2), and studies of how validation design and transfer
between areas change the apparent skill of susceptibility models for other hazards (Dasarwar and
Hindayar 2026, https://doi.org/10.1007/s11069-026-08481-3, and Mohebzadeh et al. 2024,
https://doi.org/10.1007/s11069-023-06377-0). Our manuscript asks the same questions for wildfire
across four countries and cites these studies.
