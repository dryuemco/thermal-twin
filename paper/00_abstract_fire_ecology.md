# Abstract

**Background** Wildfire susceptibility maps are usually evaluated in their training region, although agencies with limited fire records may need to use maps developed elsewhere. We tested whether pre-fire satellite thermal predictors, expected to track fuel dryness, improve cross-region transferability. Six land surface temperature (LST) and dryness predictors were added to a terrain, land-cover and greenness baseline. Random forests were trained separately in each of five regions, each represented by one major fire season (2021 or 2022): Manavgat and Muğla (Türkiye), Bejís (Spain), North Evia (Greece) and Montiferru (Italy). Performance was measured using spatially blocked area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) against Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) burned areas, within regions and across all 20 ordered source–target pairs without target labels.

**Results** Evaluating models on the burn scar and its 2 km collar reduced ROC-AUC by 0.160 [0.090, 0.230] on average compared with region-wide evaluation. With 5 km spatial blocking, thermal predictors improved within-region ROC-AUC by 0.045–0.148, but gains were small across 20 transfer directions (+0.007 [−0.020, +0.038]) and comparable evaluation areas (+0.024 [−0.004, +0.049]). Two label-free adaptation methods moved transfer performance closer to chance, and none of the 20 similarity measures was shown to predict transfer. The most similar region pair failed to transfer in either direction at point estimates. In four of five regions, hotter surfaces were associated with less burning, with interval support in two. A direct indicator of fuel dryness would show the opposite sign, so LST appears to act here as a descriptor of vegetation, soil and terrain.

**Conclusions** Skill measured in one region does not show that a susceptibility map will work in another. A model trained on one region and fire season should be tested against burned areas in the target region before it guides prevention planning there, and the evaluation area should be reported. With one fire season per region, region and event effects cannot be separated, so several seasons per region are needed to test whether these findings hold beyond single events.
