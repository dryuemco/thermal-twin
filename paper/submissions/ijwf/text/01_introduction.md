# Introduction

Wildfire is a major disturbance in Mediterranean landscapes, and climate change is altering fire regimes [@Pausas2021]. Mediterranean fire policy needs to shift from suppression towards prevention and landscape management [@Moreira2020], and susceptibility maps support this planning. Most maps follow one workflow [@Jain2020], in which predictors and burned-area records train a classifier, most commonly a random forest [@Oliveira2012; @Tonini2020], that is then evaluated in the same region. Agencies with few local fire records may have to use a model trained elsewhere.

Such transfer is rarely measured. Test folds usually come from the training area, season or fire. Random folds can inflate performance through spatial autocorrelation [@Roberts2017; @Ploton2020], while spatial blocking reduces this optimism [@Valavi2019; @Meyer2018], although its suitability for map accuracy assessment remains debated [@Wadoux2021]. Neither blocking nor the area of applicability [@Meyer2021] establishes performance on an unseen fire. Transferability requires testing on spatially or temporally independent data [@Wenger2012] and is still an open problem in ecological modelling [@Yates2018].

Few studies directly assess the transferability of fire models. Weather-driven fire-danger indices transferred poorly between fire environments in Peru [@Podschwit2022]. Regional fire-occurrence models transferred well across the Alps and the Mediterranean Basin only under similar conditions, and a cross-regional model performed better in the transfer tests [@Bekar2020]. The closest Mediterranean study [@Dimarco2026] evaluated a full transfer matrix across four countries using 500 m predictors, and all transfers achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC, hereafter AUC) of at least 0.80. Its predictors described static place characteristics, and its response was human-caused ignition. A random forest susceptibility model covering thirteen eastern Mediterranean and southern Black Sea countries was trained on a decade of fire records [@Trucchia2023], but a joint fit does not test transfer to a region without a fire record. 

When a model is transferred to a new region, its predictor distribution may change (covariate shift). Methods such as covariance alignment (CORAL) [@Sun2016] are designed to reduce this shift without target labels. The predictor-response relationship may also change (concept shift) [@MorenoTorres2012], and detecting a reversal in association requires target labels. We found no previous application of covariance alignment to fire susceptibility or burned-area prediction.

Satellite measurements of the land surface before a fire are a third kind of predictor. They change from season to season like weather, but they describe each location like a map. Static predictors change little between years, so they cannot explain why one summer burned and another did not. Satellite thermal data can be used to estimate fuel moisture [@Yebra2013], because land surface temperature (LST) rises as vegetation dries. LST combined with a vegetation index was used by Chuvieco et al. [@Chuvieco2004] to estimate live fuel moisture for fire-danger rating. The Temperature-Vegetation Dryness Index (TVDI) [@Sandholt2002] is an internally normalised form of this approach, so one would expect it to transfer better than raw temperature. Pre-fire LST anomalies are related to the burned area and duration of later fires [@Maffei2018; @Maffei2021], and pre-fire evaporative stress from spaceborne thermal data has been used to predict unseen fires within one region [@PascoliniCampbell2025]. Satellite fuel-moisture models are, however, usually site-specific, and their transfer is rarely tested [@Marino2024]. To our knowledge, the transfer of a classifier that uses pre-fire thermal state to another region, without target labels, has not been tested.

The score of a model also depends on where it is evaluated. In species distribution modelling, a larger evaluation area adds more easy absences and raises the AUC [@Lobo2008], an effect that was described in general terms as the accessible area [@Barve2011]. We found no wildfire study that keeps the model fixed and changes only the evaluation cells, although region-wide scores are often read as if they described performance next to the fire.

If transfer cannot be assumed, it might still be predicted from the similarity of two regions. Species distribution studies disagree on this, because transferability decreased with geographic distance and extrapolation in one study [@Rousseau2022] and did not in another [@Vesk2021].

We measured how far the skill of pre-fire thermal predictors transfers between five Mediterranean regions, each with one large fire season, and asked three questions. First, how much does the evaluation area change the score of one fixed model? Second, does within-region thermal skill transfer to another region without target labels? If the link between moisture stress and burning is general, thermal predictors should keep at least part of their value in a new region, and normalised channels such as the TVDI should transfer best. Third, can the similarity of two regions predict transfer, so that a source region could be chosen before a fire? We also tested two label-free adaptation methods, pooled training and the removal of predictors whose association reversed between regions. The Methods describe the regions, the classifier and the evaluation frames, the Results follow the three questions, and the Discussion considers what the scores measure and mean for fire management.
