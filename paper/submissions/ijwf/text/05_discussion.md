# Discussion

The three findings depend on each other. This section considers what the scores and the thermal predictors measured, the implications for fire management and the limitations.

## What the scores measure

The first finding sets the size of the second. When the study areas were made comparable, three between-region results changed, namely mean transfer, which elevation reversals have interval support, and the correlation of one similarity measure with transfer. Two results remained. Transfer stayed well below within-region skill, and the elevation association of Manavgat stayed reversed against other regions, although the supported pairs changed between the frames and the two collar reversals did not survive multiplicity correction. The third finding offers no way around the second, because no similarity measure was shown to predict transfer on the original study areas.

Inside one region, skill was close to chance 10 to 20 km from the training cells. The within-region reference therefore measures interpolation, how well a model fills gaps inside an observed fire season, whereas transfer is extrapolation to a new one. Part of the transfer shortfall may thus be a matter of distance, and a model of this kind is best described as having short-range skill.

Each region also contributed one fire season, dominated by one large fire. The negative class therefore contains cells that could have burned but were not reached, and ignition, wind and suppression decided much of this. Burned-area patterns have different controls at different scales [@ParisienMoritz2009], and a pre-fire surface predictor can only rank cells by their condition before the fire. The shortfall therefore cannot be split into a region effect and an event effect, and a second Muğla fire examined for this purpose gave a confounded comparison (Supplementary Section S3.5). Neither intervention changed this, because pooling helped only Evia and feature removal brought a local cost with no measured transfer gain in return.

## What the thermal predictors measured

The thermal predictors were chosen as a measure of pre-fire dryness, but they did not behave as one. At the point estimates, hotter surfaces burned less in four of five regions, and in Manavgat once elevation was held, although the Manavgat result has interval support only on the collar. In summer, LST is strongly shaped by canopy cover, exposed soil and terrain. Dense, cooler vegetation carries more fuel, so a negative association fits LST acting as a descriptor of the land surface, whereas a dryness index should show the opposite sign. In Montiferru, most of the within-region thermal gain disappeared once terrain aspect was added, which fits this reading. In Mediterranean ecosystems, the link between fire and climate also depends on fuel and productivity [@PausasPaula2012], so the sign of a surface predictor may differ between landscapes. Whether a fire becomes very large depends mostly on fire weather acting on drought-stressed fuel [@Ghasemiazma2026], which no surface predictor measured before the fire can carry. The surface component tested here would be added to weather-driven danger rating [@Vitolo2020], as in products that combine susceptibility with pre-season weather [@Bergonse2021].

Dimarco et al. [@Dimarco2026] found good transfer in a Mediterranean transfer-matrix design, while transfer failed here. Their predictors are static attributes of a place and their response is a record of human-caused ignitions. Here the predictors describe the surface in one season, and the response is the footprint of one fire season per region. These two differences cannot be separated, but the static baseline also did not transfer here, so predictor class alone does not explain the difference.

## Implications for fire management

Region-wide susceptibility maps are used for prevention planning, and these results suggest four changes in practice.

First, report the evaluation frame and the question it answers. On the same model, the region-wide and scar-frame scores differed by 0.160 AUC with the region as the unit, more than the thermal gain within most regions (Table 2), so both are needed to judge a model.

Second, measure transfer in the target region. A within-region score does not show how a model will work elsewhere, and no similarity measure tested here could replace the measurement. On average, a model moved to a new region did not usefully rank the burned cells there, although in the best direction the highest-scored 10 % of cells contained 27 % of the burned cells (Supplementary Table S1). A record of earlier burns is the only local label available before a fire. In an exploratory test, the AUC of the transferred models against the burns of the five previous years (mean 0.454) did not predict their AUC against the event (ρ = −0.39 [−0.77, +0.15], Supplementary Section S4.6).

Third, fix the study area by a stated rule before the predictors are computed, because the study areas used here were not comparable. An accessible-area rule [@Barve2011] is one option.

Fourth, do not expect label-free adaptation to repair transfer. Both methods tested moved most directions toward chance, including those that had worked. A reversed association cannot be seen without target labels, so aligning the predictor distributions cannot correct it.

## Limitations

Each region contributes one fire season, so region and event effects cannot be separated, and models trained on multi-year fire inventories were not tested. The study areas were not comparable either. They could be restricted but not extended, because they were fixed earlier in the processing pipeline. All labels come from one burned-area product, MCD64A1, with known omission and commission errors [@Boschetti2019; @Katagis2022], which are concentrated at scar edges. With VIIRS VNP64A1 labels, which overlap MCD64A1 by 87 % to 98 %, no conclusion changed, but independent fire perimeters were not used. The land-cover map is post-fire for four events. With the 2020 map [@Zanaga2021], mean transfer was 0.513 instead of 0.527 and no conclusion changed, although in Montiferru the 2021 map left out about a fifth of the burned natural vegetation (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Section S1.1). No weather predictors were used, and only four estimators were compared. The thermal predictors are two-month medians of a few morning scenes, so they describe the seasonal state of the surface and cannot follow fuel moisture in the days before a fire.

The full frame comparison is defined on seven scars in three regions, so the region-level estimates should be quoted. Single directions moved by up to 0.049 across random-forest seeds and software versions, which the direction-level intervals do not include (Supplementary Section S5.7). Spatial dependence reaches beyond the 5 km blocks, so intervals at 5 km blocking may be too narrow, especially in North Evia. The sign of the thermal contribution to transfer also depends on the estimator. It was +0.007 for the random forest used here but negative for the three simpler models (−0.011 to −0.021), although every interval includes zero.

The similarity tests rest on ten region pairs that share five regions, so both positive and negative results have low power. The reversal test is marginal. A reversal of a single predictor can also come from different predictor distributions, so it does not prove a change in the predictor-burning relationship. Finally, the low transfer into Manavgat is located but not explained. Weather, quality screening and the study area were examined, and none explained it. Terrain was held through elevation and aspect only, and in Manavgat burn timing and elevation are confounded (Supplementary Sections S1.1 and S3.3).
